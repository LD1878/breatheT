What WTA 6-4 asks of a system

Understanding the guidance behind moisture-conscious internal insulation, and what a compliant specification has to show.

20 November 2026 ● 8 min read ● BreatheTherm

WTA 6-4 is a German technical guideline for planning internal wall insulation, taking account of moisture movement, driving rain, condensation and drying potential. It considers the complete wall build-up rather than individual insulation materials. The related WTA 6-5 guideline addresses numerical hygrothermal assessment. BreatheTherm follows these principles when developing suitable specifications. PerliTherm and PerliScratch also correspond to separately WTA-certified renovation-plaster products. The point is how insulation and moisture interact, not simply how low a U-value can go.

What the WTA is and why conservation officers cite it

The WTA (Wissenschaftlich-Technische Arbeitsgemeinschaft für Bauwerkserhaltung und Denkmalpflege) is the German scientific association for building maintenance and monument preservation. Its Merkblätter, technical information sheets, are how that research reaches specifiers.

WTA Merkblatt 6-4 is the sheet most often named in UK heritage work because it deals with internal insulation of historic walls. It is not a British Standard and it is not a substitute for listed building consent. It is the language conservation officers and conservation engineers already use when they want moisture taken seriously.

If an officer asks whether a system is WTA-compliant, they are asking whether the wall has been thought through as a drying construction. A product data sheet with a lambda value is not an answer.

What WTA 6-4 requires of an internal insulation build-up

The guideline starts from the wall that exists, not from a catalogue lining. Masonry type, thickness, rain exposure, existing plasters, salts, indoor humidity and heating all belong in the assessment.

It then asks whether the proposed internal insulation still leaves the wall able to dry. Vapour movement, capillary movement and drying potential are part of that question. A lining that is excellent in the laboratory can fail the guideline if it is the wrong lining on that elevation.

Condensation is considered at the surfaces and within the build-up. Driving rain is considered on the outer face, because internal insulation makes that face colder. Junctions matter: windows, floors, beams. A perfect wall plane with a failed reveal is not a successful system.

This is whole-wall thinking. It is the same discipline as walls that are allowed to dry, written as guidance rather than as a product story.

WTA 6-5 and when hygrothermal numbers are required

WTA 6-4 tells you what to consider. WTA 6-5 is the numerical assessment of heat and moisture over time. That is the calculation people mean when they say hygrothermal modelling.

You do not model every cottage by default. You model when the case is not obvious: severe exposure, unusual thickness, mixed historic fabric, high internal moisture load, or an officer who has asked for evidence. U-values without trapping moisture is the companion explanation of why a U-value alone never answers that request.

A site-specific model is only as good as the wall data going into it. Guessing the masonry, skipping the pointing, or ignoring an existing cement render will make a tidy graph of the wrong building.

Certified renovation plasters versus a whole-wall specification

WTA also certifies products, including renovation plasters designed to work with damp masonry. That certification is about the material behaving as claimed: strength, porosity, vapour transport.

PerliTherm and PerliScratch correspond to that renovation-plaster family. They are lime and perlite, vapour-open, and used as coats on masonry rather than as a sealed board. That is necessary. It is not sufficient.

A WTA-certified bag applied as part of a vapour-closed stack, or applied to a wall that is still taking rain through failed pointing, is not a WTA 6-4 specification. The guideline is about the system. The product certification is one input to that system.

AeroGel is the thin mineral layer for geometry the plaster coats cannot occupy. It still has to sit in a vapour-open sequence, which is what the internal wall layer guide sets out.

It is about understanding how insulation and moisture interact, not simply achieving a U-value.

How BreatheTherm follows WTA 6-4 in specification

We specify from the wall: masonry, plaster, pointing, exposure, ventilation and junctions. That survey method is written up in reading a wall before specifying thickness.

We then choose a vapour-open build-up. PerliScratch keys to the masonry. PerliTherm does the thermal work in the plane. AeroGel is used where thickness would harm character or drying at a detail. Indoor moisture is treated as a ventilation problem as well as a wall problem.

Where the case needs numbers, we run hygrothermal modelling rather than arguing from a brochure. Listed buildings pick up listed building consent as a parallel track. WTA thinking does not replace consent. It makes the technical part of the application something an officer can respect.

Architects can request the technical pack, which includes WTA data and modelling notes. If you are at the start of a project, book a free assessment and we will say whether WTA 6-4 principles are enough on their own, or whether WTA 6-5 numbers should sit in the file.

What to put in a WTA-informed specification pack

A useful pack is short and specific. It names the masonry, the exposure, the proposed coats with thicknesses, and the drying argument. It attaches product data for PerliScratch, PerliTherm and any AeroGel layer, plus a drawing of the reveals and floor junctions.

If the case needs numbers, include the hygrothermal output and the assumptions behind it. If the building is listed, include the character note so the technical file and the consent file describe the same wall. Officers should not have to reconstruct the specification from a brochure.

What is WTA 6-4?
WTA 6-4 is a German technical guideline for planning internal insulation, taking account of moisture movement, driving rain, condensation and drying potential. It looks at the complete wall build-up, not at a single insulation product. Conservation officers in the UK often recognise it as the serious standard for historic fabric.
What is the difference between WTA 6-4 and WTA 6-5?
WTA 6-4 is the planning guideline: how to think about internal insulation and moisture as a system. WTA 6-5 covers numerical hygrothermal assessment of that system. One tells you what to consider. The other is how you calculate it when the case is not obvious.
Does WTA certification apply to a whole system or to a product?
Both, in different ways. WTA 6-4 is about the designed wall, including masonry, coats and indoor climate. Separate WTA product certifications exist for renovation plasters. PerliTherm and PerliScratch correspond to that renovation-plaster family. A certified bag still needs a competent wall specification.
Why do UK conservation officers mention WTA?
Because it is the established technical language for internal insulation of historic masonry. It gives officers a way to ask for moisture evidence instead of accepting a U-value. Citing WTA 6-4 shows the specification is being judged as a drying wall, not only as an energy upgrade.
Is BreatheTherm a WTA 6-4 system?
BreatheTherm follows WTA 6-4 principles when developing specifications: whole-wall thinking, drying potential, driving rain and condensation. PerliTherm and PerliScratch correspond to separately WTA-certified renovation-plaster products. Site-specific hygrothermal modelling is used where WTA 6-5 is the right next step.
